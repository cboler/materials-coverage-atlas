How to read this map

What is each dot?

One material: a specific compound in a specific crystal arrangement, so diamond and graphite are separate dots even though both are pure carbon. The data comes from the Materials Project, an open database at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory that uses quantum-physics simulations to predict how materials behave. @if (experimentalPct() !== null) { About {{ experimentalPct() | number: '1.0-0' }}% of these have also been made in a lab; the rest exist only as predictions. }

Why is a dot where it is?

There are no axes: left, right, up and down don't measure anything. Each material is described by about 100 traits (which elements it contains and in what proportions, plus properties such as band gap and density). An algorithm called UMAP flattens those traits onto the page so that materials with similar traits land near each other.

So neighbors are similar, and islands are families of related materials. The distance between two far-apart islands doesn't mean much.

What do the colors and marks show?

  • Color: the view picked in the buttons below; the legend explains it.
  • White rings: familiar construction materials (calcite, quartz, iron…) as landmarks.
  • Grey names: neighborhoods, named after the chemistry most common there.
  • Faint lines: density contours. Like elevation lines on a hiking map, they ring the crowded areas.
